Bengaluru · Jan–Dec 2024 · CPCB & KSPCB daily AQI

“Bangalore AQI today: 92.”
Which Bangalore?

A single city-wide number averages a traffic junction and a leafy suburb into a meaningless middle. I pulled a full year of daily AQI from every Bengaluru monitoring station I could get a clean export for, engineered six features per station, and clustered them three different ways to find the places the average hides.

  • 57.1AQI points between the worst and cleanest station, yearly mean
  • 13stations with complete 2024 coverage, from 14 exports collected
  • 500peak AQI recorded at Silk Board — the top of the scale
  • 2stations flagged as hotspots by all three algorithms

Why DBSCAN won, and where it didn't

DBSCAN takes the best silhouette (0.475) and the best Davies–Bouldin (0.597), but the reason it's the right tool here is structural rather than numerical: it is the only one of the three that is allowed to refuse. K-Means must assign every station to a cluster, so an extreme station gets absorbed into a broad “high” group and its extremity is averaged away. DBSCAN pushed 8 stations out as noise — and the points it refused to place are the hotspots.

Being straight about it: Hierarchical clustering beats DBSCAN on Calinski–Harabasz (13.34 vs 7.50), and with 13 points and 6 features every one of these metrics is fragile. This is a small-n study. The claim I'll defend is the ranking of stations, not the exact cluster count.

The consensus hotspots

Stations flagged by more than one algorithm. Agreement across methods with different assumptions is worth more than any single model's output.

    RVCE-Mailasandra and Silk Board are the only stations every algorithm agrees on. Silk Board is the one that hit AQI 500 — the ceiling of the index — and it is a junction, which is exactly what you'd predict and exactly why a city-wide mean is the wrong summary statistic.

    Most of this project was ETL

    The clustering at the end was almost the reward.

    1. 01 Ingest

      The CPCB CAAQMS portal hands you one Excel file per station in a wide day×month layout, and the layout is not consistent between stations. Each export had to be reshaped into a long-format time series before any two stations could be compared.

    2. 02 Reconcile

      14 exports collected, 13 usable: one file (Kadabesanahalli) only covers 2023, so including it would have compared different years. It's still in data/raw/ — dropped in the notebook, not hidden.

    3. 03 Engineer features

      Six features summarise a station's year: mean AQI, peak AQI, share of unhealthy days, 7-day rolling volatility, winter mean, and a linear trend slope. Level, spikiness, seasonality and direction — standardised before clustering, since AQI and a slope are not on the same scale.

    4. 04 Cluster and compare

      K-Means with the elbow method for K, Ward-linkage agglomerative, and DBSCAN with eps=0.940, min_samples=2. All three scored on the same internal metrics rather than picking the one that looked nicest.

    5. 05 Report

      A ranked hotspot report, per-model maps and a consensus view. Full output lives in results/, and the notebook runs top to bottom.

    What this does not show

    • Thirteen points is a small sample. Cluster-validity metrics on 13 observations are indicative, not conclusive. Treat the station ranking as the result and the cluster count as a modelling choice.
    • AQI is already an aggregate. It compresses PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, SO₂, CO and O₃ into one number, so a station can be dirty in a way this analysis cannot see.
    • Station siting is not controlled for. A roadside monitor and a rooftop monitor are not measuring the same thing, and I have no metadata to adjust for it.
    • No causal claim. This identifies where the air is worse, not why. Source apportionment would need a different study and different data.
    • Coverage gaps. Missing days were handled in the notebook rather than modelled; a station with sparser reporting carries slightly noisier features.
